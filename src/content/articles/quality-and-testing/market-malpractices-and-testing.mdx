## Why the Global Humic Market Requires Rigorous Quality Verification

Because humic substances are visually opaque, dark brown-to-black colloids with no single fixed chemical formula, the commercial agricultural market has historically been vulnerable to severe mislabeling and adulteration. A single gram of black industrial dye or paper-mill lignosulfonate can turn ten liters of irrigation water nearly as dark as concentrated potassium humate. Without standardized analytical verification, growers, distributors, and importers frequently purchase low-efficacy fillers at premium biostimulant prices—leading to inconsistent field performance and unfair skepticism toward legitimate humic science.

Understanding the seven most prevalent market malpractices—and insisting on [ISO 19822:2018](/glossary#iso-19822) laboratory verification—eliminates guesswork across procurement and agronomy.

---

## The 7 Most Common Humic Acid Market Malpractices

### 1. Sodium Lignosulfonate Adulteration (The Sulfur Clue: >3% vs. &lt;0.75%)
The single most widespread adulterant worldwide is **[sodium lignosulfonate](/glossary#sodium-lignosulfonate)** (or calcium/ammonium lignosulfonate), an inexpensive byproduct of the sulfite wood-pulping paper industry. Lignosulfonates are dark brown, highly water-soluble, and produce convincing foam and optical density in water. However, they are *not* humic substances: they lack the stable aromatic-carboxyl architecture of leonardite humates, decompose relatively rapidly in soil, and introduce excessive sodium and sulfur.

**How to detect it:**
- **The Elemental Sulfur Signature:** Genuine leonardite-derived potassium humate typically contains **less than 0.75% elemental sulfur** (commonly 0.2%–0.6% S). Because sulfite pulping covalently grafts sulfonic acid (`–SO₃⁻`) groups onto lignin fragments, lignosulfonate-adulterated products routinely test **above 3.0% to 6.5% sulfur** on ICP-OES elemental analysis.
- **High Sodium + Woody Odor:** Adulterated powders frequently exhibit elevated sodium (>3–7% Na) and emit a faint sweet, woody, or vanillin-like aroma when wetted with warm water.
- **Failed Acid Precipitation:** Unlike humic acid, lignosulfonates do not precipitate cleanly when acidified to pH 1.0, yet under legacy colorimetric tests they register artificially high "fulvic acid" percentages.

### 2. Raw Unoxidized Coal Dust & Bituminous Fines
Low-cost granular or "insoluble humic acid" blends sometimes substitute pulverized bituminous coal, sub-bituminous mine tailings, or unoxidized lignite fines for true leonardite. While coal dust is high in total organic carbon (often >50% C), its carbon is locked inside condensed, hydrophobic aromatic rings devoid of reactive carboxylic or phenolic functional groups. It contributes virtually zero cation exchange capacity, chelation, or root stimulation.

### 3. Colorimetric Assay Inflation ("Optical Density Gaming")
Older commercial laboratories relied on **[colorimetric assays](/glossary#colorimetric-assay)**—measuring light absorbance at 400–600 nm against a calibration curve. Because absorbance depends heavily on particle dispersion, pH, iron complexes, and unrelated dark chromophores (caramel color, synthetic dyes, tannins, blackstrap molasses, lignosulfonates), colorimetric methods routinely overstate true humic content by **2× to 5×**. A liquid containing 3% real humic acid fortified with caramel dye can easily read "15% humic acid" colorimetrically.

### 4. Ash & Silicate Inclusion in Legacy Gravimetric Tests
In traditional gravimetric precipitation (such as the older pre-2014 CDFA method), humic acid is extracted with `NaOH`, precipitated with `HCl`, dried, and weighed. However, fine silicate clay particles, amorphous silica, and coprecipitated mineral salts often trap inside the dark humic gel. If the analyst fails to combust a duplicate aliquot in a muffle furnace (loss-on-ignition at 500–600 °C) to subtract inorganic **ash weight**, inert mineral dirt is counted as "pure humic acid"—inflating reported purity by 10% to 25%.

### 5. "98% Humic Acid" Label Impossibility
Bags stamped *"98% Potassium Humate"* or *"98% Humic Acid"* rely on deceptive terminology—usually meaning *"98% water-soluble solids (on a dry basis)"* rather than 98% actual humic acid. Stoichiometrically, water-soluble potassium humate requires 8%–12% `K₂O` counterions to remain soluble, plus 5%–10% bound moisture and trace ash. Therefore, even top-tier, centrifuged leonardite potassium humate genuinely measures **65% to 82% humic acid** under ISO 19822 ash-free testing.

### 6. Industrial Sodium Humate Relabeled as Potassium Humate
Because caustic soda (`NaOH`) costs a fraction of potassium hydroxide (`KOH`), unethical brokers repackage industrial drilling-grade sodium humate into agricultural bags labeled "Potassium Humate." As detailed in our [Raw Materials & Extraction Guide](/raw-materials/raw-materials-and-extraction), applying high-sodium humate degrades soil aggregate stability and accelerates clay dispersion in irrigated soils.

### 7. Molasses, Vinasse, & Citric Acid Sold as "Fulvic Acid"
True concentrated fulvic acid is expensive to isolate from leonardite or humic ores because native fulvic fractions typically represent only 2%–10% of the ore. Consequently, many inexpensive "100% Fulvic Acid" powders on the market are actually fermented sugarcane molasses, yeast vinasse, corn-steep liquor, or potassium humate blended with citric acid. While molasses can feed soil microbes as a simple carbohydrate, it lacks the persistent metal-chelation stability and polyphenolic chemistry of true hydrophobic fulvic acid.

## Analytical Testing Comparison: ISO 19822 vs. IHSS vs. CDFA vs. Colorimetric

Recognizing that conflicting test methods undermined global trade and regulatory oversight, Dr. Richard Lamar, soil chemists from the USDA-ARS, and the Humic Products Trade Association (HPTA) developed a unified multi-laboratory validated protocol published as **ISO 19822:2018** (also adopted by AAPFCO in the United States and recognized under EU FPR 2019/1009).

## Inside the ISO 19822 (Lamar) Protocol: Why DAX-8 Resin Changed the Industry

The decisive breakthrough of ISO 19822:2018 is its two-stage purification architecture:

1. **Ash-Free Humic Acid Gravimetry:** After alkaline extraction (`0.1 M NaOH`) and centrifugation to remove insoluble humin and coarse mineral grit, the supernatant is acidified with `HCl` to pH 1.0 to precipitate humic acid. The washed humic precipitate is dried, weighed, and then ashed in a muffle furnace. Subtracting the residual inorganic ash weight yields the exact **ash-free humic acid mass**.
2. **Hydrophobic Fulvic Acid Separation via DAX-8 Resin:** The remaining acidic supernatant contains genuine fulvic acid mixed with non-humic water-soluble impurities (simple carbohydrates, low-molecular-weight organic acids, inorganic salts, and vinasse). Passing this solution through a column packed with non-ionic **Superlite DAX-8 acrylic ester resin** selectively adsorbs true hydrophobic fulvic acid onto the resin beads while hydrophilic sugars and salts wash straight to waste. The purified fulvic fraction is then eluted, neutralized, quantified via organic carbon/gravimetric ash correction, and reported separately.

> **Key Takeaway for Procurement & Agronomists:** Because ISO 19822 strips away ash, moisture, potassium counterions, and non-humic fillers, an ISO 19822 Certificate of Analysis will almost always display a *lower headline percentage* than a legacy colorimetric certificate for the exact same product. A verified **68% ISO 19822 potassium humate** is dramatically purer and more agronomically potent than an unverified "95% colorimetric" powder.

## A 5-Point Buyer Quality Verification Checklist

Before approving any commercial humic or fulvic batch for field use or distribution, request the following five analytical documents from an accredited third-party laboratory:

1. **ISO 19822:2018 Test Report:** Separate ash-free figures for Humic Acid (%) and Hydrophobic Fulvic Acid (%).
2. **Elemental ICP-OES Profile:** Confirm `K₂O` (8–12% for K-humate), low Sodium (`Na < 1.5%`), and **Total Sulfur (`S < 0.75%`)** to rule out sodium lignosulfonate and `NaOH` extraction.
3. **Water Insoluble Matter (WIM):** Below 1.5% for drip-fertigation and foliar grades; verify via a 120-mesh screen rinse test.
4. **Heavy Metal Compliance:** Arsenic (As), Cadmium (Cd), Chromium (Cr), Lead (Pb), Mercury (Hg), and Nickel (Ni) within [EU FPR 2019/1009 and AAPFCO thresholds](/regulations/global-organic-laws-and-standards).
5. **Physical Jar Solubility & pH Check:** Standard 1% w/v potassium humate dissolves smoothly at pH 9.0–10.5 with minimal settling after 2 hours and no sweet woody/molasses odor.
